Building an Internal AI Champions Program

By the Authority Solutions® Editorial Team | Published: April 2026 | Last Updated: April 2026

Sustaining AI Adoption Through Internal Champions

Formal AI training events build initial capability, but the skill development that produces lasting organizational transformation happens between training sessions - in daily work situations where employees encounter real problems and need practical guidance on applying AI tools to solve them. Without a support structure that bridges formal training events and daily application, skill adoption decays within weeks as employees revert to familiar pre-training workflows.

The AI Champions model addresses this gap by designating one to two team members per department as advanced AI practitioners who serve as embedded support resources for their colleagues. Champions are not managers or IT staff - they are peer-level practitioners who happen to have deeper AI proficiency, making them approachable resources for colleagues who might hesitate to ask IT for help with a prompt or management for permission to try a new tool.

The Champion Role: What It Is and What It Is Not

Champions are practical skill coaches, not theoretical instructors. Their value comes from solving real problems: a marketing coordinator struggling to get consistent brand voice from AI content drafts asks the department champion for help refining their prompt template. A sales rep unsure whether to trust a lead score asks the champion to explain how the scoring model evaluates their specific pipeline. An operations analyst wanting to automate a report generation workflow asks the champion to review their automation logic before deployment.

Champions are not responsible for strategic AI decisions (that remains with leadership), tool procurement or licensing (that remains with IT), or organizational AI governance policy (that involves legal and compliance). Their scope is tactical: helping colleagues apply AI tools effectively within the boundaries the organization has already established.

Selecting the Right Champions

Champion selection should prioritize three qualities over raw technical skill.

Curiosity and Experimentation Orientation

The best champions are the team members who naturally explore new tools, test different approaches, and share discoveries with colleagues without being asked. They are the people who already email the team "I found a way to cut report generation from 2 hours to 20 minutes using this prompt" - before the champion program exists. Curiosity drives continuous learning, which is essential because AI tools evolve rapidly and champions must stay current to remain valuable resources.

Communication and Teaching Ability

Technical proficiency without communication skill produces a champion who can solve problems but cannot transfer the solution to others. The ideal champion explains concepts in accessible language, demonstrates techniques through practical examples, and adjusts their communication approach based on the colleague's skill level. A champion who responds to every question with technical jargon frustrates rather than helps - patience and clarity are non-negotiable qualities.

Organizational Credibility

Champions need enough professional standing that colleagues trust their guidance. A junior team member who recently joined the organization may have strong AI skills but lacks the organizational context and peer credibility to influence behavioral change. Mid-level practitioners with established track records, departmental knowledge, and collegial relationships make the most effective champions because their recommendations carry implicit endorsement from demonstrated professional competence.

Champion Training and Development

Champions receive training beyond the standard organizational AI curriculum. Their advanced development covers three additional areas.

Advanced prompt engineering techniques including chain-of-thought prompting, few-shot learning, systematic prompt iteration, and domain-specific prompt optimization. Champions should be able to construct effective prompts for any task within their department's scope and diagnose why a colleague's prompt is producing suboptimal results.

Tool evaluation methodology including how to assess new AI tools against organizational governance requirements, how to conduct practical capability tests, and how to make grounded recommendations about tool adoption without being swayed by vendor marketing. Champions often serve as informal tool scouts - identifying new capabilities that could benefit their department.

Coaching and facilitation skills including how to structure one-on-one coaching interactions, how to facilitate group learning sessions (brown bag lunches, team workshops), and how to create documentation (prompt templates, workflow guides, best practice examples) that scales their knowledge beyond direct interactions.

Operating the Champions Program

Weekly Office Hours

Each champion holds a 30 to 60-minute weekly office hours session where colleagues can bring real work challenges and receive guided coaching on applying AI tools. Office hours should be informal and low-pressure - a standing invitation rather than a mandatory meeting. The format works best as drop-in sessions where colleagues bring their laptops and work through problems in real time with the champion's guidance.

Monthly Knowledge Sharing

Champions from across departments convene monthly to share discoveries, discuss challenges, and cross-pollinate techniques. A marketing department champion who discovered an effective approach to AI-assisted competitor analysis might share the methodology with the sales department champion, who adapts it for sales intelligence purposes. This cross-departmental sharing prevents knowledge silos and accelerates organization-wide AI capability development.

Prompt Template Maintenance

Champions own their department's section of the organizational prompt template library. They add new templates as effective prompts are developed, retire templates that become outdated as AI tools evolve, and refine existing templates based on usage feedback. The prompt library is the champions program's most tangible deliverable - a growing repository of tested, department-specific AI workflows that any team member can use regardless of their individual AI proficiency level.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much time should champions dedicate to the role?

Plan for 2 to 4 hours per week: 1 hour for office hours, 30 minutes for prompt library maintenance, 30 minutes for self-development, and 1 to 2 hours for ad hoc colleague support. This time investment should be formally acknowledged in the champion's workload expectations - adding champion responsibilities without adjusting other expectations creates burnout and resentment.

Should champions receive additional compensation?

At minimum, formal recognition - the champion role should appear in performance evaluations and be acknowledged as a development opportunity. Some organizations provide stipends ($100 to $500 per month), professional development budgets for AI conferences or advanced training, or preferential consideration for promotion based on demonstrated leadership in the champion role. The investment in champion recognition and compensation is negligible compared to the organizational value the program produces.

How many champions does an organization need?

One to two per department or per 15 to 20 employees, whichever produces higher coverage. A 50-person organization with 4 departments needs 4 to 8 champions. A 200-person organization needs 10 to 15. The ratio must ensure that every employee has accessible champion support within their immediate team - champions in a different department or building are significantly less effective than those embedded in the daily working environment.
